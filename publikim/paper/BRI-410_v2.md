# BRI-410: A 512-bit Tweakable Block Cipher with Key- and Tweak-Dependent Permutations

**Rinald Cobaj**, Independent researcher, vlorarino@gmail.com

*Paper version 2 (2026-10-27), describing cipher version BRI-410 v1.1. BRI-410 is a study cipher: it has not been analysed by independent cryptanalysts and must not be used to protect real data. Code, test vectors and all analysis scripts: https://github.com/rinaldcobaj-cell/BRI-410*

## Abstract

BRI-410 is an experimental tweakable block cipher with a 512-bit block, a 410-bit key, a 128-bit tweak and 16 rounds. It comes with BRI-410 AE, an instantiation of the ΘCB3 authenticated-encryption mode with a 416-bit tag. BRI-410 is a key-alternating substitution–permutation network on an 8×8 array of bytes. It uses an 8-bit S-box affine-equivalent to the AES S-box and an 8×8 Cauchy MDS matrix. Its distinctive feature is the permutation layer: in every round, the key and the tweak select a cell permutation from a family of 32 "knight-move" permutations, all of which are diffusion-optimal in both directions. We prove that the wide-trail bound of 81 active S-boxes over four rounds holds for every sequence of permutations from this family. For individual differential characteristics and linear trails, this gives at least 324 active S-boxes over 16 rounds. A CP-SAT model confirms the values for one to five rounds. This bound is not a proof of the overall security of BRI-410. All round keys and permutations are derived from the key and the tweak. Version 1.1 adds a Davies–Meyer feed-forward after tweak injection, intended to make the per-block derivation one-way, because in version 1.0 three consecutive round keys of a single block revealed the master key in 0.1 seconds. That one-wayness is a conjecture, not a proven property. We document this weakness and the other negative results of the design process, among them a predecessor with 4-bit cells that we broke over 5 rounds. We report experiments in five categories of cryptanalysis, keep proofs, computations, experiments and conjectures apart, and list open problems for independent analysis. We do not claim any practical advantage over existing designs.

## 1. Introduction

The rules of chess are public, yet a serious game almost never repeats. BRI-410 starts from this analogy: the algorithm is public, and the key, a "game", is secret. In addition, every encrypted block plays its own game, derived from the key and a per-block tweak. The 8×8 board fixes the state. The knight's move gives the permutation layer. The key length comes from Allis's estimate of about 10^123 games of chess [All94], since log2(10^123) ≈ 408.6. **This choice is symbolic.** We do not claim that security above 256 bits has practical meaning, and our security target (Section 9) is lower than 2^410.

The aim of this paper is not to propose a replacement for existing ciphers. It describes BRI-410 completely enough that independent cryptanalysts can analyse it, challenge it and, if possible, break it. It also records every weakness we found during the design.

**Contributions.**

1. A complete specification of BRI-410 v1.1, a 512-bit tweakable block cipher with a 410-bit key and 128-bit tweak, and of BRI-410 AE (Sections 3 and 4).
2. A proof that the two-round (9) and four-round (81) wide-trail bounds on individual differential characteristics and linear trails hold for every key- and tweak-dependent choice of the round permutations, both for the cipher and for the key schedule (Section 5).
3. A record of the design process with its negative results. These include a useless parameter (α), a key-dependent "game" that hides only one bit per round at row level, a 4-bit-cell predecessor broken over 5 rounds, and an invertible key schedule in version 1.0, together with the change that addresses it (Sections 7 and 10).
4. Experiments in five categories: differential, linear, algebraic, key schedule, and chosen plaintext/tweak (Section 6). Open problems and concrete targets for analysis are in Section 12.

**Kinds of evidence.** Every statement is labelled:

- **[Experiment]** a statistical or sample-based measurement
- **[Estimate]** a complexity estimate that was not implemented

- **[Open]** a question we did not study

## 2. Related work and positioning

**Tweakable block ciphers.** Liskov, Rivest and Wagner introduced tweakable block ciphers [LRW02]. The TWEAKEY framework [JNP14] processes the key and the tweak with a light, linear schedule. It underlies SKINNY [BJK+16] and Deoxys-BC [JNPS21], which have 64- and 128-bit blocks and extensive public analysis. Threefish [FLS+10], the block cipher of Skein, is the closest design in size: it has 256-, 512- and 1024-bit blocks and a 128-bit tweak, but it is an ARX design (addition, rotation, XOR) with 72 rounds for the 512-bit block.

**Wide-block SPNs.** Rijndael supports 256-bit blocks [DR02]. Kalyna [OGK+15] is an SPN standard with 512-bit blocks and keys, 8-bit S-boxes and 8×8 MDS matrices over GF(2^8), but without a tweak.

**Key-dependent components and rekeying.** Blowfish [Sch93] and Twofish [SKW+98] derive S-boxes from the key. Deriving a fresh key for every message or block is a known technique, analysed for example in [AB00]. The Davies–Meyer construction from hash functions adds a feed-forward so that the output is believed hard to invert when the underlying permutation is strong [PGV93].

| Cipher | Block | Key | Tweak | Rounds | Structure | Tweak processing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| AES-256 | 128 | 256 | — | 14 | SPN, 8-bit S-box, MDS | — |
| Kalyna-512/512 | 512 | 512 | — | 18 | SPN, 8-bit S-boxes, MDS | — |
| Threefish-512 | 512 | 512 | 128 | 72 | ARX | added to subkeys |
| SKINNY, Deoxys-BC | 64 / 128 | (tweakey) | (tweakey) | 14–56 | SPN | linear TWEAKEY schedule |
| **BRI-410 v1.1** | **512** | **410** | **128** | **16** | **SPN, 8-bit S-box, MDS** | **full re-derivation of all round keys and permutations, intended to be one-way (Davies–Meyer, not proven)** |

**Positioning.** BRI-410 brings no gain in efficiency. Re-deriving the whole schedule for every block is slower than TWEAKEY (Section 11). What we study is a different design point: a permutation layer that depends on the key and the tweak, chosen in every round from a family that is diffusion-optimal for every choice. As a result, the classical wide-trail bounds hold uniformly over all keys and tweaks. To our knowledge this exact combination has not been described before. Key-dependent components and tweak-dependent round keys as such are well known.

## 3. Specification of BRI-410 v1.1

### 3.1 Notation and parameters

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Block | 512 bits = 64 bytes. Byte i is cell (r, k) = (⌊i/8⌋, i mod 8) of an 8×8 board; row r = bytes 8r..8r+7 |
| Key | 410 bits, given as 52 bytes little-endian whose 6 top bits are 0 |
| Tweak | 128 bits (16 bytes) |
| Rounds | R = 16 |
| Field | GF(2^8) with polynomial x^8 + x^4 + x^3 + x + 1 |

### 3.2 Components

**S-box S.** `S(x) = A·x^-1 ⊕ c` (with 0^-1 = 0). Here `A` is an invertible 8×8 binary matrix: row i is a byte, and bit i of `A·y` is the parity of `A[i] AND y`. `c` is a byte. `A ‖ c` are the 9 bytes of `SHAKE-256("BRI-410 S-box8 v1" ‖ n)`, where n is the first 4-byte big-endian counter that gives an invertible A and an S-box without points S(x) = x or S(x) = x ⊕ FF. The result is n = 9. The table is in Appendix A.

**Permutation family π.** For (dr, dk) ∈ D = ((1,2), (1,−2), (−1,2), (−1,−2)), in this order, and β ∈ {0,…,7}:

```
π_(d,β):  cell (r, k)  →  cell (r + k·dr,  k + k·dk + β)   (mod 8)
```

Cell (r, k) makes k knight jumps (dr, dk) on a toroidal board, then the board shifts β columns. This gives 32 permutations, all distinct.

**Linear layer θ.** Each row is multiplied by the 8×8 matrix `M[i][j] = 1 / (x_i ⊕ y_j)`, where x = (0,…,7) and y = (145, 234, 150, 206, 98, 226, 235, 94), the first 8 distinct bytes outside {0,…,7} of `SHAKE-256("BRI-410 MDS8 v1")`. M and M^-1 are in Appendix A.

### 3.3 Encryption and decryption

```
X ← P ⊕ RK_0                             (whitening with RK_0, Section 3.4)
for i = 1 .. 16:
    X ← θ( π_i( S(X) ) ) ⊕ RK_i          (S on all 64 bytes; π_i moves S(X)[c] to cell π_i(c))
C ← X
```

Decryption applies `⊕RK_i, θ^-1, π_i^-1, S^-1` for i = 16, …, 1, and finally `⊕RK_0`. All rounds are identical. The last round also applies θ.

### 3.4 Key and tweak schedule ("the game")

The schedule state G has 80 bytes (10 rows of 8). One step is

```
F_c(G):  S on all 80 bytes → θ on each of the 10 rows → byte j moves to position 9·j mod 80 → byte 79 ⊕= c
```

Version 1.1 computes:

```
L   ← key (bits 0..409) ‖ first 230 bits of SHAKE-256("BRI-410/8 mbushja") as little-endian (bits 410..639)
K*  ← F_0xF3(F_0xF2(F_0xF1(F_0xF0(L))))            key only; the same for every tweak
X   ← K* with bytes 0..15 XORed with the tweak
G_0 ← F_0xFB(…F_0xF4(X)…) ⊕ X                      8 steps and a Davies–Meyer feed-forward
RK_0 ← bytes 0..63 of G_0                           the whitening key of Section 3.3
for i = 1 .. 16:
    G_i ← F_i(G_(i−1))
    RK_i ← bytes 0..63 of G_i
    π_i  ← π_(D[b & 3], (b >> 2) & 7), where b = byte 64 of G_i
```

So there are 17 round keys, RK_0 to RK_16, and 16 permutations, π_1 to π_16. The schedule cost is (each step evaluates 80 S-boxes):

| Process | Steps | S-boxes |
|---|---|---|
| Key preparation K*, once per key and shared by all tweaks | 4 | 320 |
| Tweak mixing, per tweak | 8 | 640 |
| Round-key generation, per tweak | 16 | 1 280 |
| Schedule for one tweak, without the shared preparation | 24 | 1 920 |
| Schedule for the first tweak under a key, including the preparation | 28 | 2 240 |

With the 1 024 S-boxes of the 16 rounds, the first block under a key costs 3 264 S-boxes, and every further block, which reuses K*, costs 2 944. The feed-forward is a design measure meant to make the map from G_0 back to X hard to invert. **We do not prove that it is one-way** (Conjecture 2 in Section 9).

**Version 1.0** (Sections 6 to 8, history) used 4 steps after the tweak and no feed-forward: `G_0 = F_0xF7(…F_0xF4(X)…)`, which is 4 + 16 = 20 steps per tweak after the shared preparation, or 24 including it. The round function is the same in both versions.

## 4. BRI-410 AE

BRI-410 AE instantiates ΘCB3 [KR11] with BRI-410/8 v1.1 as the tweakable block cipher E. Each call uses the tweak `N ‖ ((index << 4) | type)`: a 12-byte nonce N, then 4 bytes little-endian, with index < 2^28. The types are 0 message block, 1 mask of the final partial block, 2 tag without a partial block, 3 tag with a partial block, 4 associated-data block, and 5 final partial associated-data block. Associated data uses N = 0^12. With M = M_1 … M_m M_* and A = A_1 … A_a A_* (|M_*|, |A_*| < 64 bytes), and pad(x) = x ‖ 80 ‖ 00…:

```
C_i   = E^(N,i,0)(M_i)                                                          i = 1..m
if M_* is not empty:  C_* = M_* ⊕ E^(N,m,1)(0^512) truncated;   F = E^(N,m,3)(⊕M_i ⊕ pad(M_*))
otherwise:            F = E^(N,m,2)(⊕M_i)
Auth  = ⊕ E^(0,i,4)(A_i)   [⊕ E^(0,a,5)(pad(A_*))]
Tag   = first 52 bytes of (F ⊕ Auth)                                            416 bits
Packet = N ‖ C ‖ Tag
```

Decryption returns nothing unless the tag verifies, and the comparison is constant-time. The correspondence with ΘCB3 was checked by the author against the description of the mode, not by an independent party. **[Open]**

## 5. Structural results

**Lemma 1 (permutation family). [Proof]** Each π_(d,β) is a bijection, and it is diffusion-optimal in both directions: the 8 cells of any row go to 8 distinct rows, and the 8 cells that land in any row come from 8 distinct rows.

*Proof.* The column map k → k(1 + dk) + β is a bijection of Z_8, since 1 + dk ∈ {3, −1} is odd. Given the column, the row map r → r + k·dr is a bijection, so π is a bijection. For a fixed source row r, the destination rows r + k·dr (k = 0..7) are distinct because dr = ±1 is invertible mod 8. For a destination row q, every column k occurs exactly once among its cells, because the column map is a bijection. The source row of the cell with column k is q − k·dr, and these are distinct. ∎

**Lemma 2 (the row shift α adds no structure). [Proof]** Let T_α be the row rotation (r, k) → (r + α, k). Then T_α commutes with S, which acts cell by cell, with every π_(d,β), since both sides give (r + α + k·dr, k(1+dk) + β), and with θ, which applies the same matrix to every row. Hence a round-dependent T_α inside the round can be moved to the end of the cipher, replacing RK_i with T(RK_i). So it only adds a final rotation of rows that depends on the key. We therefore removed α (in an earlier version, measurements found 1 792 pairs of "game" variants that were equivalent up to α). ∎

**Lemma 3 (MDS). [Proof + Computation]** Every square submatrix of a Cauchy matrix is a Cauchy matrix, and its determinant is non-zero when the x_i are distinct, the y_j are distinct and x_i ≠ y_j [MS77]. Hence M is MDS and its differential branch number is 9. Since the dual of an MDS code is MDS, the linear branch number is also 9. We also checked all 12 869 square submatrices of M and of M^-1: none is singular. M has no column symmetry other than the identity.

**Theorem 1 (bounds for every permutation sequence). [Proof]** Consider any differential (or linear) trail through r rounds of BRI-410 with a non-zero input, any key and any tweak. Then:
(a) any two consecutive rounds contain at least 9 active S-boxes, and
(b) any four consecutive rounds contain at least 81 active S-boxes,
for every sequence of permutations π_i from the family.

*Proof.* Let a_t be the number of active bytes at the input of the S-layer of round t, and let ρ(·) be the number of active rows.
(a) θ_t acts row by row with branch number 9. For each active row at its input, active input bytes plus active output bytes ≥ 9. The input bytes of θ_t are the S-outputs of round t, moved by π_t. Its output bytes are the S-inputs of round t+1, since adding the key does not change activity. Summing over rows gives a_t + a_(t+1) ≥ 9 · ρ(input of θ_t) ≥ 9.
(b) Take rounds 1 to 4. By (a), a_1 + a_2 ≥ 9·ρ_1 and a_3 + a_4 ≥ 9·ρ_3, where ρ_t is the number of active rows at the input of θ_t. Let q be any active row at the input of θ_2. It has w_in active bytes, and its image row at the output of θ_2 has w_out bytes, with w_in + w_out ≥ 9. The w_in bytes come, through π_2, from w_in distinct rows of the state at the input of round 2 (Lemma 1, inverse direction). Each of these rows is an output row of θ_1, so it is also active at the input of θ_1, hence ρ_1 ≥ w_in. The w_out bytes of row q are S-inputs of round 3, and π_3 sends them to w_out distinct rows (Lemma 1, forward direction), hence ρ_3 ≥ w_out. Therefore a_1 + … + a_4 ≥ 9(ρ_1 + ρ_3) ≥ 9(w_in + w_out) ≥ 81. The argument for linear trails is the same, with masks propagating through the transpose of θ, which also has branch number 9 (Lemma 3). ∎

**Corollary.** 4k rounds contain ≥ 81k active S-boxes. 16 rounds contain ≥ 324.

**Theorem 2 (key and tweak schedule). [Proof + Computation]** The permutation j → 9j mod 80 of the schedule sends the 8 bytes of each of the 10 rows to 8 distinct rows, and receives each row from 8 distinct rows [Computation]. The proof of Theorem 1 therefore applies to the steps F_c. Any non-zero difference in the schedule state, in particular a difference in the tweak, activates at least 81 S-boxes in 4 steps and at least 162 in the 8 steps of v1.1 that precede RK_0.

**What these bounds guarantee, and what they do not.**

- They **guarantee** that every single differential characteristic, or linear trail, activates at least the stated number of S-boxes, for every key, every tweak and every choice of permutations. With the maximal S-box probabilities of 2^-6, this means that any single 4-round trail has probability ≤ 2^-486 and any 16-round trail ≤ 2^-1944, **under the usual assumption of independent round keys**.
- They **do not guarantee** anything about (i) differentials, where many trails add up, (ii) linear hulls, (iii) the actual dependencies between round keys produced by the schedule, or (iv) attacks of other kinds: integral, algebraic, boomerang, impossible-differential, or related-key beyond the schedule bound.
- In particular, Theorems 1 and 2 are **not a proof of the security of BRI-410 as a whole**.

**Computation. [Computation]** A CP-SAT model at the level of active bytes, in which the adversary may choose the row structure of π_i in every round (Appendix C), returned the minimum number of active S-boxes: 1, 9, 17, 81 and 82 for 1 to 5 rounds (proven optimal), and 1, 9, 17, 81, 107 and 114 for 1 to 6 schedule steps. For 6, 7 and 8 rounds it found trails with exactly 90, 98 and 162, which equal the bounds that follow from Theorem 1. These values were computed once (OR-Tools 9.15, 2026-10-22). Later runs on the author's machine were blocked by an operating-system application-control policy, so an independent re-run is welcome. The proofs above do not depend on this computation.

## 7. The key-schedule weakness of v1.0 and the change in v1.1

### 7.1 The weakness

In v1.0 every schedule step is a bijection, and the steps after the tweak have no feed-forward. **Given three consecutive round keys RK_i, RK_(i+1), RK_(i+2) of one block, and its tweak, the master key follows immediately. [Experiment]**

1. RK_i gives bytes 0..63 of G_i. These determine the outputs of θ on rows 0..7 of step i+1, so 13 of the 16 bytes 64..79 of G_(i+1) are known. Bytes 70, 71 and 79 remain unknown.
2. RK_(i+2) gives 6 byte equations on row 8 and 7 on row 9 of G_(i+1). An exhaustive search over 2^16 and 2^8 values gives a unique G_(i+1).
3. The steps F_i are inverted back to G_0. The 4 tweak-mixing steps are undone, the tweak is removed, and the 4 key-only steps are undone. The 230 known padding bits confirm the key.

In 3 out of 3 trials the master key was recovered in about 0.1 s, with a single candidate. With **two** consecutive round keys, 3 bytes remain unknown: 2^24 candidates, each verifiable through the padding bits **[Estimate]**. Once the key is known, the round keys of **every** other tweak follow. The per-block "game" therefore did not isolate blocks. This weakness does not break 16 rounds, since no method is known to obtain round keys there. It does mean that, in v1.0, any attack recovering two or three consecutive round keys of one block becomes a full break.

### 7.2 The change

Version 1.1 adds a Davies–Meyer feed-forward after the tweak injection and doubles the number of steps there from 4 to 8: G_0 = F(X) ⊕ X, with F made of 8 steps and X = K* ⊕ tweak. The state G_0 of an attacked block can still be reconstructed. But going from G_0 back to X now requires inverting the map X → F(X) ⊕ X, a preimage problem on 640 bits. The step-by-step inversion of Section 7.1 no longer applies, because the feed-forward mixes the input into the output. **Whether the map can be inverted by other means is not proven either way**: the feed-forward is a design measure, and its one-wayness is Conjecture 2. The round function is unchanged.

### 7.3 Evaluation

| Attack of 7.1 (3 trials each) | G_0 of the attacked block reconstructed | Master key recovered | Round keys of another tweak predicted |
|---|---|---|---|
| v1.0 | 3/3 | **3/3** | **3/3** |
| v1.1 | 3/3 | **0/3** | **0/3** |

Cost: v1.1 is 13% slower than v1.0 in our implementation (2 295 against 2 651 blocks per second, with a new tweak for every block and the key preparation K* shared).

**This is not a proof.** The three failures show only that the direct inversion of Section 7.1 no longer works. They do not show that F(X) ⊕ X cannot be inverted by other methods, such as meet-in-the-middle or rebound techniques of the kind applied to AES-based hashing modes [Sas11]. Our hypothesis is stated as Conjecture 2.

## 8. Reduced-round attacks (fixed tweak)

**Lemma 4 (3-round integral distinguisher). [Proof + Experiment]** Under a fixed tweak, take 256 plaintexts in which one byte takes all values and the rest is constant. After 3 rounds, every one of the 64 output bytes sums to zero.

*Proof.* After round 1, the row that receives the active byte contains 8 bytes of the form c ⊕ m·S(v) with m ≠ 0, so each takes all 256 values. In round 2, π_2 sends these 8 bytes to 8 distinct rows (Lemma 1). Each row then has one byte that takes all values and 7 constant bytes, so after θ all 64 bytes take all values. In round 3, θ outputs XOR combinations of such bytes, so every output byte sums to zero. Adding the key keeps this property. ∎

Measured: 10 out of 10 trials balanced after 2 and 3 rounds. After 4 rounds 0–1 of 64 bytes were balanced, and 0–1 also with two aligned bytes (2^16 texts). With a fresh tweak per plaintext the property disappears **[Experiment]**.

**4-round key recovery. [Experiment]** Lemma 4, plus guessing each byte of θ^-1(RK_4) separately (since θ^-1(C) = π_4(S(X_3)) ⊕ θ^-1(RK_4) and every byte of X_3 is balanced), recovered RK_4 (512 bits) in 5 out of 5 trials with 512–768 chosen plaintexts and in under one second. **This attack recovers only RK_4, not the master key.** Under v1.0, the master key would follow from three consecutive round keys of one block, or from two with about 2^24 work (Section 7.1). Removing round 4 with the recovered RK_4 and attacking the remaining rounds in the same way might also give RK_3 and RK_2, but we did not implement this. Whether a 4-round master-key recovery on v1.0 is practical therefore remains **[Open]**. Under v1.1, round keys of one tweak do not lead to the master key by the direct method of Section 7.1.

**5 rounds. [Estimate]** Adding one more round requires guessing 8 bytes of θ^-1(RK_5) and one byte of θ^-1(RK_4) per target byte: about 2^72 guesses with 2^8 texts, plus the unknown row structure of π_4 and π_5. Done naively, this is about 2^80 S-box evaluations. Partial-sum techniques may reduce it. We did not implement this attack, and the estimate may be wrong in either direction.

**Margin.** The best attack we executed covers 4 of 16 rounds. Our rule of thumb R ≥ 2·max(R1, R2, R3), a heuristic and not a proof, is satisfied. Here R1 = 2 rounds to full diffusion, R2 = 6 rounds until the best trail is below 2^-512 (by Theorem 1), and R3 = 4–5 rounds attacked, so 2·6 = 12 ≤ 16.

## 9. Security claims and conjectures

**What is proven:** Lemmas 1–4, Theorems 1 and 2 and their corollaries, as stated, including their limits (Section 5).

**Conjecture 1 (security target).** In the single-key setting, with chosen plaintexts, ciphertexts and tweaks (repeated tweaks included), 16-round BRI-410 v1.1 has no distinguishing or key-recovery attack with time below 2^256 and data below 2^128 blocks per key. This is a research hypothesis and an invitation to analysis, **not a demonstrated result**. We deliberately set this target far below the key length, and the key length remains 410 bits. The 410-bit key is a symbolic choice, and our analysis covers only the attack classes of Sections 5–8.

**Conjecture 2 (tweak isolation).** The map X → F(X) ⊕ X with its 8 steps is one-way in practice. As a result, knowing all round keys and permutations for one tweak gives no practical advantage in computing those of another tweak or the master key. This is a hypothesis supported only by the design rationale and by the failure of the direct attack (Section 7.3).

**BRI-410 AE.** If Conjecture 1 holds, the security theorem of ΘCB3 [KR11] gives confidentiality and authenticity against nonce-respecting adversaries. The forgery probability per attempt is about 2^-416 plus the advantage against the cipher. Limits: messages of at most 2^28 − 1 blocks. With random 96-bit nonces, at most 2^32 messages per key, which keeps the probability of a repeated nonce around 2^-33.

**Not claimed:**
- security against related keys
- security of BRI-410 inside a hash function (known or chosen key)
- resistance to nonce reuse in BRI-410 AE (a repeated nonce reveals equal blocks)
- protection of the reference code against timing and other side channels (it is not constant-time)

## 11. Reproducibility and performance

The repository contains:
- the research implementation (Python, standard library)
- a separate single-file reference implementation in English, which agrees bit for bit with the research implementation
- test vectors for v1.0 and v1.1
- 46 unit tests
- every experiment script, with fixed seeds

The CP-SAT model requires OR-Tools. In pure Python the implementation runs at about 2 300 blocks per second with a new tweak for every block and the key preparation K* shared, and BRI-410 AE at about 150 KB/s. A constant-time implementation in C is an open task.

## 12. Open problems and targets for analysis

1. **Differential and linear:** clustering of differentials, linear hulls, boomerang and impossible-differential attacks; re-running the CP-SAT bounds independently.
2. **Algebraic and integral:** division-property analysis for 4 or more rounds, and algebraic attacks on reduced rounds.
3. **Key schedule:** preimages of X → F(X) ⊕ X with 8 steps (Conjecture 2); related-key attacks; whether reduced-round attacks lead to the master key.
4. **Tweak:** related-tweak attacks that combine tweak and plaintext differences, including pairs whose permutations differ.
5. **Mode and implementation:** an independent check of the ΘCB3 instantiation, and constant-time implementations.

**Targets.** Distinguishers for 5, 6, 7 or 8 rounds; recovery of round keys for 5 to 8 rounds; recovery of the master key, or of the round keys of a second tweak, from those of one tweak, in either the fixed-tweak or the chosen-tweak setting. The author will acknowledge every result publicly.

## Appendix C. The CP-SAT model

The variables are x[t][c] ∈ {0,1}: cell c is active at the input of the S-layer of round t. For every round t and row q, one binary variable d[t][q] marks the row as active. Every active input or output cell of the row implies d[t][q], and the constraint is (active inputs of θ_t in row q) + (active outputs x[t+1][8q..8q+7]) ≥ 9·d[t][q]. The inputs of row q are the cells c with r + k·dr ≡ q, where c = (r, k). A binary variable s[t] selects dr = +1 or −1 in round t, so the solver chooses the worst case. The objective minimises Σ x[t][c] over rounds 1..R, subject to Σ x[0][c] ≥ 1. The model of the schedule is the same, with 10 rows and the map j → 9j mod 80, and it starts from a non-zero difference in bytes 0..15 (the tweak). Code: `matje/f4b_milp.py`.
